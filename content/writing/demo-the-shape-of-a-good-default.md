A default is rarely neutral. It is the path most people will take, the state
screenshots will preserve, and the behavior future maintainers will treat as
normal.

Good defaults tend to share a few properties:

- They are safe to try without reading recovery instructions.
- They reveal enough of the system to make the next choice understandable.
- They remain useful after the first ten minutes.
- They can be changed without leaving hidden state behind.

The tempting default is often the impressive one. It fills the screen, enables
every integration, and demonstrates the full vocabulary of the product. The
better default is usually quieter. It gives the user a stable place from which
to make one deliberate change.

This is why configuration is part of interface design. The first value is not
just a value; it teaches people what the software considers ordinary.
